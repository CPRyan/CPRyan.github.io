research

Past, present, and future of my research on aging and health across the lifecourse.

Biological Aging and Geroscience

My research program is increasingly centered in geroscience: the study of biological processes that shape aging, resilience, and risk for age-related disease. I use high-dimensional omics data, clinical laboratory data, cohort studies, trials, and computational statistics to develop, implement, and validate measures of biological aging.

I am Analysis Project Lead for FAST: Finding Aging biomarkers by Searching existing Trials, an ARPA-H supported initiative focused on identifying biomarkers of aging across existing human trials. This work fits directly into my broader goal of building biological aging measures that can be used across interventions, cohorts, and clinical settings.

Examples from my work include the CALERIE DNA methylation analysis, which found that caloric restriction slowed DunedinPACE in healthy adults, and the CALERIE Genomic Data Resource, a multi-tissue, multi-omic resource for translational geroscience. I also work on adapting Pace of Aging methods for population studies of healthspan and lifespan, and on evaluating whether biological aging measures can improve risk stratification for cognitive decline and other aging-related outcomes.

The Cebu Longitudinal Health and Nutrition Survey remains an important foundation for this work. Cebu is where I developed much of my approach to population epigenomics, life course epidemiology, and the use of biological aging measures in diverse human cohorts.

Reproduction, Stress, and Resilience

My second major research area asks how physiological stress is recorded in biological systems and how those signatures relate to aging, resilience, and disease risk. I use stress broadly, including reproduction, pregnancy, psychosocial adversity, caloric restriction, and acute clinical events such as major surgery.

In Cebu, my colleagues and I showed that pregnancy is linked to faster epigenetic aging in young women, while related work has examined immune-cell and DNA methylation differences during pregnancy and lactation. I am especially interested in pregnancy as a model of both aging and rejuvenation: a period of intense physiological demand that may reveal mechanisms of vulnerability, repair, resilience, and later-life health.

This research extends into current work on maternal stress and fetal/placental epigenetics, chronic stress biology in CLHNS, and planned projects on adverse pregnancy outcomes and long-term women’s health. I am also developing work that uses electronic health record laboratory measures to study biological reserve, recovery, and resilience after cardiothoracic surgery.

Together, these projects connect my earlier work on reproduction, inequality, and life history theory to a broader program on physiological stress and resilience across the life course.
